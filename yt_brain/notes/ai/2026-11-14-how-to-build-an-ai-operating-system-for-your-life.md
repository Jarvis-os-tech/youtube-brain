## Key Points
- Peter Yang (ex-Roblox PM, now full-time creator) runs a one-person business on a cohesive AI system built from reusable “skills” (saved instruction sets) shared across ChatGPT, Claude Code, and Grok Bot.
- Three life/business systems: a chief of staff (daily triage of email/calendar/Slack), an advisor (checks decisions against yearly goals), and a council (multiple AI perspectives on big decisions).
- Content systems cover podcast guest research, interview guides, titles/thumbnails, video editing, intros, and repurposing one recording into newsletter + social posts.
- Scheduled tasks deliver business stats, fitness check-ins, and weekend family activity ideas.
- A “skill editor” skill maintains and cleans up other skills.
- Harness matters more than model: ChatGPT for browser/computer use, Grok Bot for scheduled tasks (runs on a VM) and X research, Claude for writing voice, Codex/ChatGPT for coding.
- The advisor runs on a human-written one-page plan (goals, what he’s not doing, energy, drains, family) — a “slop-free zone” the AI never edits.
- Skills are improved iteratively by reviewing multiple conversations together rather than overfitting to one.
- AI handles short-form video well but long-form still needs human editors; Peter built a timestamped feedback UI (like Frame.io) for agent edits.
- Start with one recurring task rather than building everything at once.
- Peter delivers recurring AI summaries by email because he actually reads email; examples include a weekly business review, weekend kid-activity search, and fitness summaries.
- Instead of one giant AI, he gives separate bots specific jobs, each with a clear purpose, needed inputs, and a definition of “good.”
- A worked example: a “Bass coach” project with custom instructions and a Google Drive practice log synced via MCP, producing a weekly check-in and a simple practice-logging app.
- Peter’s producer bot researches breaking topics on X and compares them against YouTube coverage to find content gaps.
- Human judgment still matters: AI can draft/clean up, but the human reviews changes (as numbered before/after lists) and always checks user-facing or customer-facing content.
- Peter still does not use AI for episode titles; he works with producers/editors on those.

## Required / Actionable Items
- Define reusable skills for recurring tasks so you don’t re-explain context each time.
- Write a personal plan doc yourself (goals, non-goals, energy, drains, family) and keep AI out of editing it.
- Connect Google Drive/Docs (and email/calendar/Slack) connectors so skills can read them.
- Set up a chief of staff scheduled task (e.g., 8:30 a.m. weekdays, cloud run, push notifications) that triages email/calendar/Slack into a numbered priority list with links and suggested next steps; drafts emails but never sends without approval.
- Run the advisor about once a month against the plan doc.
- Improve skills by reviewing several recent threads at once, then apply repeated feedback via the skill editor.
- For podcast prep, provide guest, topic, audience, and format; request sources, angles, and build-on-each-other questions.
- Never let the AI send emails on its own; always review drafts.
- When building a bot, define its purpose, required inputs, and success criteria; for recurring bots, also set when it runs and where results are sent.
- Sync context sources (e.g., paste a Google Drive URL into the project’s context) so the bot can keep watching them.
- Schedule recurring check-ins (example: Sunday 6:30 p.m.).
- Review AI edits as a numbered before/after list and reject any that don’t make sense; keep a human pass on anything user-facing.
- Start small: pick one workflow, give it good context (like a one-page plan), use it, and improve it over time.
